# Reading list for the paper

*One entry per paper we cite or decide on: a link, what the paper **shows** (its result, not only
its topic), and where paircompte uses it (decision number, module, document). Read the ones marked
★ first — a decision rests on them. State: paircompte 0.2.0, 2026-09-12. Every DOI below was
checked in PubMed (2026-09-11 for the entries of the first draft, 2026-09-12 for the rest); an
entry PubMed could not confirm would say "[DOI to verify]" — none does today. Two entries have no
DOI by nature (a book, a software URL).*

## Truth sets and the human–chimpanzee reference case

- ★ **Mills RE, Bennett EA, Iskow RC, Luttig CT, Tsui C, Pittard WS, Devine SE (2006).** Recently
  mobilized transposons in the human and chimpanzee genomes. *Am J Hum Genet* 78:671–679.
  <https://doi.org/10.1086/501028> — Comparing the 2005 assemblies copy by copy, with curation,
  they find ~11,000 TE copies differentially present between human and chimpanzee, > 95 % of them
  Alu, L1 and SVA; the human lineage carried more transposition, and the two lineages amplified
  different subfamilies. Target-site-duplicated empty sites are their criterion for an insertion
  polymorphism. **Used as:** the per-copy truth set of the paper (PAPER_PLAN § 2.1,
  `scripts/truth_lift.py`, D5); the empty-site criterion of the target-site test (D39). **To do:**
  obtain the supplementary tables (PMC1424692; assemblies hg17 and panTro1 [conf < 80 %, to confirm
  from the Methods]), convert them to the `truth_lift.py` layout and lift them through
  hg17→hg19→hg38 and panTro1→…→panTro6 chains.
- **Chimpanzee Sequencing and Analysis Consortium (2005).** Initial sequence of the chimpanzee
  genome and comparison with the human genome. *Nature* 437:69–87.
  <https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04072> — 1.23 % single-nucleotide divergence between the two
  genomes, ~1.5 % of each genome in lineage-specific indels; the lineage-specific TE insertion
  counts of both lineages, with Alu activity several-fold higher in the human lineage. **Used as:**
  the divergence and the assembly background of the reference case (§ 2.11 of the draft).
- **Hedges DJ, Callinan PA, Cordaux R, Xing J, Barnes E, Batzer MA (2004).** Differential Alu
  mobilization and polymorphism among the human and chimpanzee lineages. *Genome Res* 14:1068–1075.
  <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.2530404> — Human-specific Alu insertions are dominated by the AluYa5
  and AluYb8 subfamilies, chimpanzee-specific ones are fewer and belong to other Alu Y lineages;
  recent Alu activity differs in rate and in subfamily between the two lineages. **Used as:** the
  family-level expectation of chr21 and of the whole-genome human × chimpanzee run (draft § 3.3, 3.4).
- **Lee J, Cordaux R, Han K, Wang J, Hedges DJ, Liang P, Batzer MA (2007).** Different evolutionary
  fates of recently integrated human and chimpanzee LINE-1 retrotransposons. *Gene* 390:18–27.
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gene.2006.08.029> — The chimpanzee-specific L1 subfamily L1Pt and the
  human-specific L1HS (L1PA1) arose after the split and took different paths; L1PA2 predates it.
  **Used as:** the family-level expectation (L1Pt chimpanzee-only, L1HS human-only, L1PA2 mixed).
- **Wang H, Xing J, Grover D, Hedges DJ, Han K, Walker JA, Batzer MA (2005).** SVA elements: a
  hominid-specific retroposon family. *J Mol Biol* 354:994–1007.
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmb.2005.09.085> — SVA is a composite element (SINE-R, VNTR, Alu-like)
  of ~2,700 copies in the human genome, in six subfamilies (SVA_A–F) of which the youngest, SVA_E
  and SVA_F, are human-specific. **Used as:** the family-level expectation; the reading of the
  45 % lineage-specific SVA fraction genome-wide (draft § 3.4).

## Background — why per-copy

- **Cordaux R, Batzer MA (2009).** The impact of retrotransposons on human genome evolution. *Nat
  Rev Genet* 10:691–703. <https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg2640> — Retrotransposons make up ~40 % of the
  human genome; Alu, L1 and SVA are still active, and their insertions, recombination and
  transduction shape genome structure and cause disease. **Used as:** the introduction's framing of
  a TE copy as a dated, positioned mutation with a known mechanism.
- **Platt RN 2nd, Vandewege MW, Ray DA (2018).** Mammalian transposable elements and their impacts on
  genome evolution. *Chromosome Res* 26:25–43. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10577-017-9570-z> — A
  review of mammalian TE diversity: most mammals are dominated by L1 and lineage-specific SINEs, a
  few lineages (bats) carry recent DNA-transposon and rolling-circle invasions, and TE activity
  differs sharply between sister lineages. **Used as:** introduction (lineage-specific activity as
  the rule).
- **Sotero-Caio CG, Platt RN 2nd, Suh A, Ray DA (2017).** Evolution and diversity of transposable
  elements in vertebrate genomes. *Genome Biol Evol* 9:161–177. <https://doi.org/10.1093/gbe/evw264>
  — Across vertebrates, TE content and composition vary widely, with mammals TE-rich and
  L1-dominated and birds TE-poor; the review lays out the classes and their vertebrate
  distribution. **Used as:** introduction; the reason birds were dropped from the pair list.
- **Lander ES, et al. (2001).** Initial sequencing and analysis of the human genome. *Nature*
  409:860–921. <https://doi.org/10.1038/35057062> — ~45 % of the human genome is TE-derived; the
  divergence of each copy from its family consensus dates it, which gives the age distribution of
  the repeat classes (the first "repeat landscape") and shows most human TE activity to be ancient.
  **Used as:** the basis of the age flag (D7) and of the family-level comparison the tool goes beyond.
- **Kapitonov V, Jurka J (1996).** The age of Alu subfamilies. *J Mol Evol* 42:59–65.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00163212> — Each Alu subfamily is dated from its average divergence
  from consensus, from the oldest (AluJ) to the youngest (AluY lineages), with a calibrated
  substitution rate. **Used as:** the age flag (D7) and the burst side project (PAPER_PLAN § 5b).
- **Ho JSY, Douse CH, Marazzi I (2026).** Transposable element DNA and RNA: drivers of gene
  expression, evolution, and disease. *Cell* 189:3513–3540.
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2026.05.003> — A current review arguing that the unit of TE
  biology has moved from the family to the individual locus: single copies act as promoters,
  enhancers, splice sites and RNA sources, and their effects are locus-specific. **Used as:** the
  introduction's argument for the per-copy unit (D1).

## The alternatives (positioning and benchmark)

- ★ **Groza C, Chen X, Wheeler TJ, Bourque G, Goubert C (2024).** A unified framework to analyze
  transposable element insertion polymorphisms using graph genomes (GraffiTE). *Nat Commun* 15:8915.
  <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53294-2> — GraffiTE finds polymorphic TE insertions from
  assemblies or long reads against a reference through SV callers and a graph genome, genotypes
  them with short or long reads, and reports high precision and recall on simulated and real
  data in human, fly, maize and cannabis. **Used as:** the benchmark competitor (PAPER_PLAN § 2.2);
  the argument that SV callers assume within-species flank identity (draft § 1, § 4).
- ★ **Kapusta A, Suh A, Feschotte C (2017).** Dynamics of genome size evolution in birds and mammals.
  *PNAS* 114:E1460–E1469. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1616702114> — From pairwise whole-genome
  alignments, DNA gained by TE insertion and DNA lost by deletion are of the same order in every
  lineage, so genome size stays roughly constant while its content turns over (the "accordion"
  model); the balance is read in aggregate, not per element. **Used as:** the chain-based,
  aggregate alternative (PAPER_PLAN § 2.3; draft § 1).
- **Osmanski AB, et al. (2023).** Insights into mammalian TE diversity through the curation of 248
  genome assemblies. *Science* 380:eabn1430. <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abn1430> — The
  largest TE curation to date: mammals resemble each other in total TE content but differ
  strongly in recent accumulation, with one TE type dominating at a time; young LINEs drive genome
  growth, DNA transposons go with smaller genomes. All of it at the family level, without
  positions. **Used as:** what family-level comparison gives and does not give (draft § 1, § 4).
- **Kent WJ, Baertsch R, Hinrichs A, Miller W, Haussler D (2003).** Evolution's cauldron:
  duplication, deletion, and rearrangement in the mouse and human genomes. *PNAS* 100:11484–11489.
  <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1932072100> — Introduces the chain/net construction behind UCSC's
  whole-genome alignments and liftOver, and counts, per megabase of human–mouse alignment, two
  inversions, 17 duplications, seven transpositions and 200 deletions ≥ 100 bp; a chain gap is
  unaligned sequence in one genome with no confidence stated per site. **Used as:** the chain
  baseline (D37, `scripts/chain_baseline.py`), the truth lifting (`scripts/truth_lift.py`), and the
  statement of what a chain leaves implicit (draft § 1, § 2.10).
- **Gardner EJ, et al. (2017).** MELT. *Genome Res* 27:1916–1929.
  <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.218032.116> — Discovers and genotypes mobile-element insertions from
  discordant and split reads of population-scale short-read data (the 1000 Genomes call set), one
  species against its own reference. **Used as:** the introduction's map of read-based callers.
- **Zhuang J, Wang J, Theurkauf W, Weng Z (2014).** TEMP. *Nucleic Acids Res* 42:6826–6838.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gku323> — Calls both presence (non-reference insertions) and absence
  (reference copies missing in the sample) from paired-end reads, with allele frequency in pooled
  samples. **Used as:** the introduction's map.
- **Yu T, et al. (2021).** TEMP2. *Nucleic Acids Res* 49:e44. <https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkab010> —
  A benchmark of germline TE-insertion callers on simulated and real data and an algorithm that
  removes false positives from chimeric reads, plus an estimate of de novo (somatic) insertion
  frequency. **Used as:** the introduction's map.
- **Kofler R, Gómez-Sánchez D, Schlötterer C (2016).** PoPoolationTE2. *Mol Biol Evol* 33:2759–2764.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msw137> — TE insertion frequencies from Pool-Seq by a "physical
  pile-up" of read pairs, comparable across populations without a per-sample reference. **Used as:**
  the introduction's map.
- **Nelson MG, Linheiro RS, Bergman CM (2017).** McClintock. *G3* 7:2763–2778.
  <https://doi.org/10.1534/g3.117.043893> — Integrates several read-based TE callers into one
  pipeline and shows on yeast that their calls only partly overlap, i.e. that the method matters.
  **Used as:** the introduction's map.
- **Orozco-Arias S, et al. (2020).** TIP_finder. *Biology (Basel)* 9:281.
  <https://doi.org/10.3390/biology9090281> — Detects TE insertion polymorphisms from discordant
  reads at population scale on HPC hardware. **Used as:** the introduction's map.

## Components and their behaviour

- ★ **Li H (2018).** Minimap2: pairwise alignment for nucleotide sequences. *Bioinformatics*
  34:3094–3100. <https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/bty191> — Minimizer seeds chained into
  candidate alignments, then base-level extension with preset scoring; the `asm` presets score
  +1/−4 (asm20 for ~20 % divergent assemblies), `map-ont` +2/−4, and the `sr` preset is tuned for
  short reads. **Used as:** the aligner of every level; the preset scoring behind the cliffs of
  D34; the short-read preset for exon mapping (D30, D42, D46); the score floors lifted for short
  queries (D25).
- **Storer J, Hubley R, Rosen J, Wheeler TJ, Smit AF (2021).** The Dfam community resource.
  *Mob DNA* 12:2. <https://doi.org/10.1186/s13100-020-00230-y> — Dfam 3 admits both curated
  families and uncurated RepeatModeler-derived families with their taxonomic scope stated, and
  ships genome annotations built from them; for a clade without a curated library there is little
  or nothing to mask with. **Used as:** why family names are library names (draft § 4); why the
  GenArk masking of the octopuses is empty (D45, draft § 3.9); the clade export of
  `paircompte-annotate --dfam-taxon`.
- **Flynn JM, Hubley R, Goubert C, Rosen J, Clark AG, Feschotte C, Smit AF (2020).** RepeatModeler2.
  *PNAS* 117:9451–9457. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1921046117> — Adds the structural LTR
  pipeline (`-LTRStruct`) to RepeatModeler and recovers most of the curated families of the three
  benchmark genomes (fly, zebrafish, rice) automatically; runtime is days per genome on 16 cores.
  **Used as:** the de novo step of the annotation route (D45, `docs/ANNOTATION.md`); the EC2 sizing
  (D36).
- **Hubley R, et al. (2016).** The Dfam database of repetitive DNA families. *Nucleic Acids Res*
  44:D81–D89. <https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkv1272> — Dfam 2.0: profile HMMs per family, which
  annotate more of the human genome than the consensus library alone. **Used as:** the annotations
  the tool consumes (UCSC RepeatMasker tracks).
- **Smit AFA, Hubley R, Green P (2013–2015).** RepeatMasker Open-4.0.
  <http://www.repeatmasker.org> — The masker whose `.out` files are paircompte's copy definitions
  (software; no DOI). **Used as:** input format (`paircompte/io.py`), the masking step of D45.
- **Wong WY, Simakov O (2019).** RepeatCraft. *Bioinformatics* 35:1051–1052.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/bty745> — Merges the fragmented loci RepeatMasker reports
  for one element (LTR + internal region, elements broken by nested insertions) into whole elements
  using the annotation and LTR structure. **Used as:** the prior art for family unification
  (D31/D33) and the split-host case (D35).
- **Gao C, et al. (2012).** Characterization and functional annotation of nested transposable
  elements in eukaryotic genomes. *Genomics* 100:222–230.
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ygeno.2012.07.004> — Nested TE insertions are widespread in
  eukaryotic genomes, a host's pieces are annotated separately, and the nesting order gives a
  relative chronology. **Used as:** background for D35 and the host-dating idea (PAPER_PLAN § 5).
- **Kronmiller BA, Wise RP (2013).** TEnest 2.0. *Methods Mol Biol* 1057:305–319.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-62703-568-2_22> — Annotates nested TEs, reconstructs the order of
  insertion and dates LTR elements from their LTR divergence. **Used as:** background for the
  nesting labels and the host clock.
- **Chen J-Q, Wu Y, Yang H, Bergelson J, Kreitman M, Tian D (2009).** Variation in the ratio of
  nucleotide substitution and indel rates across genomes in mammals and bacteria. *Mol Biol Evol*
  26:1523–1531. <https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msp063> — The substitution/indel ratio is ~8–12 in
  mammalian non-coding DNA, 5.4× higher in coding than non-coding sequence, and rises with
  divergence; indels concentrate where constraint is low. **Used as:** the indel rate of the
  simulated ladder (D38, `--indel-frac 0.1`).
- **Craig NL, Chandler M, Gellert M, Lambowitz AM, Rice PA, Sandmeyer SB (eds) (2015).** *Mobile
  DNA III.* ASM Press, Washington DC. (book; no DOI) — The integration mechanisms per element
  class: LINE-1 and Alu target-primed reverse transcription leaves a 7–20-bp target-site
  duplication, LTR integration a 4–6-bp one, Helitrons none. **Used as:** the TSD lengths and the
  class exceptions of the target-site test (D39).
- **van de Lagemaat LN, Gagnier L, Medstrand P, Mager DL (2005).** Genomic deletions and precise
  removal of transposable elements mediated by short identical DNA segments in primates. *Genome
  Res* 15:1243–1249. <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.3910705> — Retroelements can be excised precisely
  by recombination between their 10–20-bp target-site duplications, leaving a site
  indistinguishable from the pre-integration one; 0.5–1 % of the apparent human–chimpanzee
  "insertions" are such deletions, and 19 % of 200–500-bp deletions since the split are flanked by
  ≥ 10-bp identical repeats. **Used as:** why a clean site does not prove a gain, and why the
  target-site test reports a signature, not a verdict (D39).

## Annotation

- ★ **Goubert C, Craig RJ, Bilat AF, Peona V, Vogan AA, Protasio AV (2022).** A beginner's guide to
  manual curation of transposable elements. *Mob DNA* 13:7.
  <https://doi.org/10.1186/s13100-021-00259-7> — Step-by-step protocols for turning de novo
  consensus into a curated library: extend and re-align each family, cluster redundant models,
  remove gene-derived and multi-copy-gene consensus (the ProtExcluder logic), and state the
  library's provenance. **Used as:** the annotation protocol's filter and reporting list (D45,
  `paircompte/annotate.py`, `docs/ANNOTATION.md` § 4).
- ★ **Wicker T, et al. (2007).** A unified classification system for eukaryotic transposable
  elements. *Nat Rev Genet* 8:973–982. <https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg2165> — The hierarchical TE
  classification (class, order, superfamily, family) and the 80-80-80 rule: two elements belong to
  one family when they share ≥ 80 % identity over ≥ 80 % of their length for ≥ 80 bp. **Used as:**
  the clustering rule of the merged library (`cd-hit-est -c 0.8 -aS 0.8 -aL 0.8`, D45) and the class
  vocabulary of `--te-classes`.
- **Fu L, Niu B, Zhu Z, Wu S, Li W (2012).** CD-HIT: accelerated for clustering the next-generation
  sequencing data. *Bioinformatics* 28:3150–3152. <https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/bts565> —
  The parallelised CD-HIT: greedy incremental clustering by identity and alignment coverage with
  near-linear speed-up to ~8 cores. **Used as:** the clustering tool of the annotation route
  (`cd-hit-est`; the `.clstr` parser of `families-map`, D45).
- **Li H (2023).** Protein-to-genome alignment with miniprot. *Bioinformatics* 39:btad014.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btad014> — Spliced protein-to-genome alignment with
  k-mer sketches and vectorised dynamic programming, tens of times faster than earlier tools at
  comparable accuracy; it writes GFF with per-alignment rank and identity. **Used as:** CDS anchors
  for a genome without gene models (`paircompte-annotate --anchor-proteins`, D45): rank-1 hits at
  identity ≥ 0.9 over ≥ 80 % of the protein become the GTF the anchoring reads.
- **Kimura M (1980).** A simple method for estimating evolutionary rates of base substitutions
  through comparative studies of nucleotide sequences. *J Mol Evol* 16:111–120.
  <https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01731581> — The two-parameter distance separating transitions from
  transversions, K = −½ ln[(1 − 2P − Q)√(1 − 2Q)]. **Used as:** the divergence measure of the
  RepeatMasker landscapes the annotation route draws (`calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl`, CpG-adjusted;
  D45 step 6).
- **Albertin CB, et al. (2015).** The octopus genome and the evolution of cephalopod neural and
  morphological novelties. *Nature* 524:220–224. <https://doi.org/10.1038/nature14668> — The
  *O. bimaculoides* genome: no whole-genome duplication, expanded protocadherin and C2H2 families,
  and large genomic rearrangements associated with TE expansions; ≈ 45 % of the genome is TE.
  **Used as:** the published TE fraction the annotation summary is compared with (D45,
  `test_simple_repeats_only_is_refused`); the octopus pair background (draft § 3.9).
- **Albertin CB, et al. (2022).** Genome and transcriptome mechanisms driving cephalopod evolution.
  *Nat Commun* 13:2427. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-29748-w> — Chromosome-scale assemblies
  of *O. bimaculoides* and the squid *Doryteuthis pealeii*: coleoid genomes are highly rearranged
  relative to other molluscs, with tandem gene arrays and two distinct patterns of A-to-I editing,
  one directed at repetitive elements. **Used as:** the *O. bimaculoides* assembly and repeat
  library of the annotation route (D45); the 2022 vs 2015 sequence-set distinction that confused
  the first octopus fetch.
- **Petrosino G, et al. (2022).** Identification of LINE retrotransposons and long non-coding RNAs
  expressed in the octopus brain. *BMC Biol* 20:116. <https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-022-01303-5> —
  Full-length, transposition-competent RTE-class LINEs exist in *O. vulgaris* and *O. bimaculoides*,
  are transcribed and translated in neurons of behaviourally plastic brain areas, and generate
  germline and somatic polymorphisms. **Used as:** a published octopus TE library candidate for the
  annotation route (D45).
- **Bonar M, Elliott TA, Ahmadi MAM, Cottenie K, Linquist S (2026).** Biological implications of a
  detailed repeat annotation in *Octopus vulgaris*. *Genome Biol Evol* 18:evag137.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/gbe/evag137> — A curated repeat annotation puts ~66 % of the
  *O. vulgaris* genome in repeats (previous octopus estimates 43–50 %) and resolves four TE
  expansions at 18, 25, 33 and 56 Mya where two were known, plus a landscape of TE hot and cold
  spots. **Used as:** the *O. vulgaris* library and assembly of the annotation run; the burst ages
  on either side of the 34–44-My split for the polarisation test (PAPER_PLAN § 5b; draft § 3.9).

## Beyond the aligner's range (D47, `docs/BEYOND_THE_LIMIT.md`)

- ★ **Hamada M, Ono Y, Asai K, Frith MC (2017).** Training alignment parameters for arbitrary
  sequencers with LAST-TRAIN. *Bioinformatics* 33:926–928.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btw742> — `last-train` infers substitution and gap scores
  from the frequencies of substitutions, insertions and deletions in the data to be aligned, which
  improves alignment accuracy and reduces reference bias for reads with unusual error profiles.
  **Used as:** the third alignment tier (D47 ii, `paircompte/last.py`): the pair's own scoring
  learned on its 20 largest region pairs.
- **Frith MC, Mitsuhashi S (2023).** Finding rearrangements in nanopore DNA reads with LAST and
  dnarrange. *Methods Mol Biol* 2632:161–175. <https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-0716-2996-3_12> — The
  LAST workflow with `last-train`, `lastal` and `last-split` resolves complex sequence
  relationships (gene conversion, processed pseudogenes, chromothripsis) and is what makes reads
  of one species alignable to another species' genome; the trained probabilities are the point.
  **Used as:** the LAST command sequence of the tier (`last-train` → `lastal -p` → `last-split`) and
  the argument that trained scoring extends the direct range (D47).
- **Mouse Genome Sequencing Consortium; Waterston RH, et al. (2002).** Initial sequencing and
  comparative analysis of the mouse genome. *Nature* 420:520–562.
  <https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01262> — About 40 % of the human genome aligns to mouse at
  ~0.5 substitutions per site; ancestral repeats — TE copies inserted before the primate–rodent
  split and found at orthologous positions in both — serve as the neutral standard against which
  ~5 % of the genome is found to be under selection. **Used as:** the evidence that per-copy
  orthology of TE copies is routine for the alignable fraction at human–mouse distance (H1 of
  BEYOND_THE_LIMIT § 1; draft § 3.10); the mouse assembly background.
- **Lunter G, Rocco A, Mimouni N, Heger A, Caldeira A, Hein J (2008).** Uncertainty in homology
  inferences: assessing and improving genomic sequence alignment. *Genome Res* 18:298–309.
  <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.6725608> — At human–mouse divergence > 15 % of aligned bases in
  whole-genome alignments are wrong, through three error types that bias every algorithm
  systematically; better evolutionary models help only modestly, so alignment uncertainty must be
  carried, not averaged away. **Used as:** the caveat on every high-divergence call and on the
  bridge, whose errors are inherited and systematic (D47; draft § 2.9, § 3.10).
- **Blanchette M, Green ED, Miller W, Haussler D (2004).** Reconstructing large regions of an
  ancestral mammalian genome in silico. *Genome Res* 14:2412–2423.
  <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.2800104> — The eutherian ancestor of a 1.1-Mb region is
  reconstructed from 19 mammals at ~98 % expected base accuracy, and the reconstruction reveals
  remnants of ancient transposon insertions that direct pairwise analysis missed. **Used as:** the
  literature's first form of the bridge idea — the ancestor is halfway (BEYOND_THE_LIMIT § 2.2) —
  not implemented.
- **Paten B, Herrero J, Fitzgerald S, Beal K, Flicek P, Holmes I, Birney E (2008).** Genome-wide
  nucleotide-level mammalian ancestor reconstruction (Ortheus). *Genome Res* 18:1829–1843.
  <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.076521.108> — A transducer model infers ancestral sequences with
  substitutions *and* indels without fixing a single alignment; hold-out tests predict extant bases
  better than the closest neighbour does, and "fossilised" ancestral pseudogenes are recovered.
  **Used as:** the ancestral-reconstruction route (BEYOND_THE_LIMIT § 2.2), not implemented.
- **Armstrong J, et al. (2020).** Progressive Cactus is a multiple-genome aligner for the
  thousand-genome era. *Nature* 587:246–251. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2871-y> — Aligns
  hundreds of genomes progressively through inferred ancestral sequences (HAL ancestors), which is
  why distant pairs are comparable through the tree rather than directly. **Used as:** the same
  argument as Paten 2008; the reference multiple-aligner design the bridge avoids.
- **Braasch I, et al. (2016).** The spotted gar genome illuminates vertebrate evolution and
  facilitates human–teleost comparisons. *Nat Genet* 48:427–437.
  <https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3526> — The slowly evolving gar genome, which diverged before the
  teleost genome duplication, makes conserved non-coding elements visible between human and
  teleosts that direct comparison misses: a bridge works because the bridging lineage is slow.
  **Used as:** why the 20/20/30 triangle needs rate heterogeneity and where a bridge gains most
  (BEYOND_THE_LIMIT § 2.1; draft § 2.9).
- ★ **Doronina L, Reising O, Clawson H, Ray DA, Schmitz J (2019).** True homoplasy of retrotransposon
  insertions in primates. *Syst Biol* 68:482–493. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sysbio/syy076> —
  Screening millions of Alu insertions genome-wide, they verify nine precise parallel insertions
  at orthologous sites in two ape lineages and twelve precise deletions: homoplasy at 0.01 % of
  insertions and 0.001–0.002 % of deletions, so presence/absence at an orthologous site is a
  virtually homoplasy-free character and conflicting patterns come from incomplete lineage sorting
  or hybridisation. **Used as:** the justification of the bridge's transitive logic and of its
  `inconsistent` class (D47 iii, `paircompte/bridge.py`, `test_bridge_patterns_and_timing`).
- **Doronina L, Churakov G, Kuritzin A, Shi J, Baertsch R, Clawson H, Schmitz J (2017).** Speciation
  network in Laurasiatheria: retrophylogenomic signals. *Genome Res* 27:997–1003.
  <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.210948.116> — 102 phylogenetically informative retrotransposon
  presence/absence markers, screened genome-wide among bat, horse, cow and dog, support every
  possible topology to some degree — a network shaped by incomplete lineage sorting and ancestral
  hybridisation, read from insertion patterns and their flanking sequence. **Used as:** what
  per-copy presence/absence across three or more genomes can and cannot resolve (BEYOND_THE_LIMIT
  § 2.3); the timing patterns of the bridge.
- **Lum JK, Nikaido M, Shimamura M, Shimodaira H, Shedlock AM, Okada N, Hasegawa M (2000).**
  Consistency of SINE insertion topology and flanking sequence tree: quantifying relationships
  among cetartiodactyls. *Mol Biol Evol* 17:1417–1424.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.molbev.a026242> — The topology from SINE presence/absence
  and the tree from the flanking sequences of the same loci agree, supporting irreversible
  insertion, and the flanks date the splits that the insertion pattern alone cannot. **Used as:**

- **Shimamura M, Abe H, Nikaido M, Ohshima K, Okada N (1999).** Genealogy of families of SINEs in
  cetaceans and artiodactyls. *Mol Biol Evol* 16:1046–1060.
  <https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.molbev.a026194> — SINE families born at successive nodes

  hippopotamuses and whales; the distribution of a family among species is a phylogenetic

  *Nature* 428:493–521. <https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02426> — The rat genome (2.75 Gb) aligned to

## TE exons and splice sites (D40, `paircompte-splice`)

- **Sela N, Mersch B, Gal-Mark N, Lev-Maor G, Hotz-Wagenblatt A, Ast G (2007).** Comparative
  analysis of transposed element insertion within human and mouse genomes reveals Alu's unique role
  in shaping the human transcriptome. *Genome Biol* 8:R127.
  <https://doi.org/10.1186/gb-2007-8-6-r127> — Of 3.9 M human and 3.1 M mouse TEs, 60 % lie in
  introns in both species; all families can exonize, shared families do so at the same rate in
  both, but Alu exonizes far more, giving 1,824 TE-derived exons in human against 506 in mouse; TE
  insertions into first and last exons are commoner in human. **Used as:** the normalisation of
  the splice analysis — events per 1,000 intronic copies of the same class or family — and its
  reference counts (D40).
- **Schmitz J, Brosius J (2011).** Exonization of transposed elements: a challenge and opportunity
  for evolution. *Biochimie* 93:1928–1934. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biochi.2011.07.014> —
  Exonization proceeds stepwise over millions of years, from a weak alternative splice site to a
  constitutive exon, and can be reconstructed by comparing related species (a MIR exon, an Alu
  exon 1, an A-to-I-edited Alu). **Used as:** why the per-copy orthology of the substrate is the
  missing variable in exonization studies (D40).
- **Sibley CR, Blazquez L, Ule J (2016).** Lessons from non-canonical splicing. *Nat Rev Genet*
  17:407–421. <https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg.2016.46> — Cryptic and non-canonical splice sites,
  especially those in TE-derived sequence, are a major source of new transcripts in evolution and
  of disease transcripts, and are held in check by quality control. **Used as:** the donor/acceptor
  event classes of the splice analysis (D40).
- **Sorek R, Ast G, Graur D (2002).** Alu-containing exons are alternatively spliced. *Genome Res*
  12:1060–1067. <https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.229302> — Of 1,176 alternatively spliced internal
  human exons 61 (5.2 %) contain Alu sequence; of 4,151 constitutive ones none; 84 % of the Alu
  exons in coding regions cause a frameshift or a premature stop, and they are included at low
  levels. **Used as:** why an annotated exon inside a TE is a weak, alternative exon by default —
  the report's "New insertions and genes" counts annotated exons and says it does not measure
  inclusion (D40, extended 2026-09-12).
- **Lev-Maor G, Sorek R, Shomron N, Ast G (2003).** The birth of an alternatively spliced exon:
  3′ splice-site selection in Alu exons. *Science* 300:1288–1291.
  <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1082588> — The mechanism that chooses the 3′ splice site in
  exonized Alus, and mutations that activate a silent intronic Alu into an exon. **Used as:** the
  acceptor-in-TE event is the documented first step of exonization (D40).
- **Nekrutenko A, Li W-H (2001).** Transposable elements are found in a large number of human
  protein-coding genes. *Trends Genet* 17:619–621. <https://doi.org/10.1016/s0168-9525(01)02445-3>
  — 533 of 13,799 human genes (~4 %) carry TE sequence in their coding regions, 89.5 % of it
  recruited from introns as new exons; taxon-specific TEs make this a source of species
  divergence. **Used as:** the baseline fraction against which the per-class counts of the
  report are read (D40, extended).
- **Sela N, Mersch B, Hotz-Wagenblatt A, Ast G (2010).** Characteristics of transposable element
  exonization within human and mouse. *PLoS ONE* 5:e10907.
  <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0010907> — Exonization is biased towards the 5′ end of
  the coding sequence in both species; SNP data show exonization can be population-specific;
  Alu and non-Alu TEs differ in SNP density. **Used as:** the human–mouse comparison this
  analysis extends to per-copy orthology (D40).
- **Zhang Y, Romanish MT, Mager DL (2011).** Distributions of transposable elements reveal
  hazardous zones in mammalian introns. *PLoS Comput Biol* 7:e1002046.
  <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002046> — Intronic TEs in human and mouse are depleted
  near exons in class-specific "underrepresentation zones"; TEs within 20 bp of an exon sit
  mostly near splice donors; disease-causing and chimeric-transcript insertions show the opposite
  trend; polymorphic mouse LTR insertions in *Trpc6* and *Kcnh6* disrupt transcription. **Used
  as:** the reading of the intergenic / intronic share of new insertions against the genic share
  of the compared sequence in the report (D40, extended 2026-09-12).
- **Attig J, Agostini F, Gooding C, Chakrabarti AM, Singh A, Haberman N, Zagalak JA, Emmett W,
  Smith CWJ, Luscombe NM, Ule J (2018).** Heteromeric RNP assembly at LINEs controls
  lineage-specific RNA processing. *Cell* 174:1067–1081.
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.07.001> — Young LINEs recruit repressive RBPs (MATR3,
  PTBP1) that insulate them and the surrounding intron from splicing and 3′-end processing; as
  their motifs decay, older LINEs sit closer to exons and become a common source of
  tissue-specific, lineage-specific exons. **Used as:** why lineage-specific (young) copies carry
  fewer annotated splice sites than shared (old) ones — the expected direction of the report's
  comparison (D40, extended).
- **Merkin JJ, Chen P, Alexis MS, Hautaniemi SK, Burge CB (2015).** Origins and impacts of new
  mammalian exons. *Cell Rep* 10:1992–2005. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2015.02.058> —
  Thousands of species- and lineage-specific exons from cDNA of five mammals and a bird; most new
  internal exons derive from unique, not repetitive, intronic sequence, sit in 5′ UTRs, are
  alternatively spliced, and raise the host gene's expression where included. **Used as:** the
  caution that TE-derived exons are a minority of new exons — the report's fraction is expected
  to be small (D40, extended).
- **Krull M, Brosius J, Schmitz J (2005).** Alu-SINE exonization: en route to protein-coding
  function. *Mol Biol Evol* 22:1702–1711. <https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msi164> — 153 human
  loci with exonized Alus; four reconstructed across all primate infraorders show exonization at
  various times after insertion, sometimes lost again in some lineages. **Used as:** the
  per-lineage history that a shared copy with `exon_in_partner = no` may carry (D40).
- **Piriyapongsa J, Polavarapu N, Borodovsky M, McDonald J (2007).** Exonization of the LTR
  transposable elements in human genome. *BMC Genomics* 8:291.
  <https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2164-8-291> — LTR retrotransposons are associated with 1,057
  human genes (5.8 %), in 256 cases in protein-coding regions, 50 exons made entirely of LTR
  sequence; an IL22RA2 exon from a MaLR dated across primates. **Used as:** the LTR row of the
  per-class table has a literature counterpart (D40, extended).
